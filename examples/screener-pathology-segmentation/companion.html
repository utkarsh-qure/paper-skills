Screener

Self-supervised Pathology Segmentation in Medical CT Images

Mikhail Goncharov, Eugenia Soboleva, Mariia Donskova, Daniil Ignatyev et al. · IRA-Labs, INM, MSU, MBZUAI · ICLR 2026 · arXiv:2502.08321 · github.com/mishgon/screener

Pathology detection in 3D CT has historically been a supervised game — and a constrained one, because supervised models can only find classes that someone has labeled. Screener proposes the opposite: treat any pathology as a statistical anomaly relative to healthy tissue, and detect anomalies by learning where dense self-supervised image features fall in low-density regions. The work sits in the density-based unsupervised visual anomaly segmentation (UVAS) family, where one model produces voxel-level "descriptors" and a second learns their density given some conditioning context. Prior density-based UVAS used ImageNet-pretrained CNNs for descriptors and sinusoidal positional encodings for conditions — both fail on medical CT for different reasons. Screener replaces both: descriptors come from a 3D UNet trained from scratch via dense SSL on 30,000+ unlabeled volumes, and conditions are learned the same way plus a random masking augmentation that makes them invariant to local pathology while encoding global anatomy. The masking trick is the central trick — it simplifies $q(y \mid c)$ just enough that a diagonal Gaussian matches a Glow normalizing flow.

Going in, you should know

  • Unsupervised visual anomaly segmentation (UVAS) — detect anomalous regions without per-pixel labels by modeling "normal." Four families exist (synthetic, reconstruction, density-based, student-teacher); Screener is density-based.
  • Dense self-supervised learning — pretraining that produces per-voxel embeddings, not a global vector. Positive pairs are formed at the pixel level (same anatomical position, different augmentations). DenseCL and VICRegL are the closest references.
  • Conditional density estimation — modeling $q(y \mid c)$ instead of $q(y)$. Conditioning lets the model say "this descriptor is normal for this anatomical region" instead of just "this descriptor is rare overall."
  • Normalizing flows (Glow) — invertible neural networks for exact density via change-of-variables (act-norm + invertible 1×1 conv + affine coupling). Here, the baseline that masking-invariance lets a Gaussian replace.

§1 · What changes

The opening move of every UVAS method is the same: encode the image into features, then decide which features are surprising. In natural-image UVAS — the MVTec-AD setting that birthed CFLow-AD, MSFlow, PatchCore — the encoder is invariably ImageNet-pretrained and the surprise signal is computed from where features lie in either a density model or a memory bank. The features are good because natural images and ImageNet share a domain. Medical CT does not share that domain.

Two things follow. First, the natural-image UVAS encoders fail when transplanted: MSFlow with ImageNet ResNet50 features hits AUROC 0.71/0.67/0.63/0.63 on LIDC/MIDRC/KiTS/LiTS — a near-coin-flip on kidney and liver tumors (Table 1). Second, the obvious fix — use a medically supervised encoder — makes things worse: STU-Net, supervised on anatomical-structure segmentation, drops to 0.52/0.44/0.52/0.64 (Table 4). The supervised features collapse to organ shapes; they lose the discriminative noise needed to flag pathologies. ImageNet is bad; medically supervised is worse.

Replace the descriptor with something domain-specific and self-supervised. Replace the condition with something that captures anatomy but stays blind to the very pathology you're trying to detect.

That's the Screener thesis in one breath. The descriptor side has a clear path forward — dense SSL methods like DenseCL and VICRegL already learn pixel-level discriminative embeddings; bring them to 3D medical via a UNet trained at full resolution. The condition side is the harder problem: prior UVAS pipelines hand-craft conditions (sin-cos positional encodings, or anatomical positional embeddings) because the condition must not leak the pathology signal it's conditioning on. If the condition learns "lung tumor present here," the conditional density model has no surprise left to find. Screener's trick is to make the condition model invariant to local pathology by construction, using random masking as an extra augmentation during dense SSL.

The payoff is two-fold. Headline numbers: voxel AUROC 0.96 / 0.87 / 0.90 / 0.93 on the four eval datasets, up from 0.87 / 0.76 / 0.76 / 0.80 for the strongest UVAS baseline (Patched Diffusion). And a structural payoff: the masking-invariance simplifies the conditional density so much that a diagonal Gaussian replaces Glow as the density model — one MLP for $\mu(c)$ and $\Sigma(c)$ instead of an invertible network with act-norms, 1×1 convs, and affine couplings.

§2 · The three-model pipeline

Screener factors UVAS into three modular models, trained sequentially and combined at inference. The architecture diagram below labels each component with the paper's notation, so the math in §3 maps directly onto boxes here.

The descriptor model

$f_{\theta^{\text{desc}}}$ is a 3D UNet trained from scratch on 30,000+ unlabeled CT volumes (NLST + AMOS + AbdomenAtlas). The training objective is standard dense SSL: from a CT volume, extract two overlapping random crops, augment each independently (color jitter, no masking yet), pass both through the UNet, and form positive pairs from voxels at the same absolute anatomical position across the two views. Either DenseInfoNCE (App. B) or DenseVICReg can be used; both reach comparable performance, suggesting the dense-SSL regime matters more than the specific objective. The output is $y[p] \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at every voxel, with $d = 32$ — small enough that density modeling is tractable, large enough to retain discriminative signal.

The condition model

$g_{\theta^{\text{cond}}}$ shares every architectural and training detail with the descriptor model, with one exception: the augmentation pipeline adds random masking. A binary mask is sampled, applied to the input volume (the condition model is fed both the masked volume and the mask itself as input channels — App. F: in_channels + 1), and the dense-SSL positive-pair objective is applied as usual. Two masked views of the same volume must produce identical voxel embeddings at the same anatomical position. The training pressure: $c[p]$ must be inferable from unmasked context alone. A consequence: any local information that can be hidden by masking (pathologies, fine-grained textures) is squeezed out of $c$.

The density model

$q_{\theta^{\text{dens}}}(y \mid c)$ is the only model that trains after the others are frozen. The paper explores two parameterizations:

  • Diagonal Gaussian. $q(y \mid c) = \mathcal{N}(y; \mu(c), \text{diag}(\sigma^2(c)))$ where $\mu, \sigma$ are MLPs of $c$.
  • Glow normalizing flow. Act-norm + invertible $1 \times 1$ conv + affine coupling, with $c$ injected into the affine-coupling networks via concatenation (App. D).

Both are trained on the negative log-likelihood of descriptor/condition pairs sampled from random crops across the training set. At inference, the anomaly map is computed per-patch via $1 \times 1 \times 1$ convolutions (cheap), and overlapping patch maps are averaged into the volume-level score map.

Note that Stage 1 trains two nominally-identical UNets — one for descriptors, one for conditions — each independently for ~3 days on a single H100. The condition UNet's masking augmentation is the only place this pipeline diverges from running two copies of DenseVICReg.

§3 · The objective

Three losses, applied sequentially. The descriptor and condition models are each trained to minimize a dense-SSL objective (App. B); these are independent of the density model and run first. With both frozen, the density model is fit by minimizing conditional negative log-likelihood:

$$\min_{\theta^{\text{dens}}} \; \frac{1}{m \cdot |P|} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{p \in P} -\log q_{\theta^{\text{dens}}}\bigl(\mathbf{y}_i[p] \,\big|\, \mathbf{c}_i[p]\bigr)$$

where $(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{c}_i) = (\text{sg}[f_{\theta^{\text{desc}}}(x_i)], \text{sg}[g_{\theta^{\text{cond}}}(x_i)])$ for a random crop $x_i$, $P$ is the spatial grid of the crop, and stop-gradient on the inputs is implicit — the density model's gradients never reach $f$ or $g$.

At inference, the anomaly score at every voxel is:

$$\text{score}(p) \;=\; -\log q_{\theta^{\text{dens}}}\bigl(\mathbf{y}[p] \,\big|\, \mathbf{c}[p]\bigr)$$

The interpretation is clean: the conditional density model is a per-voxel predictive model — given the context $c[p]$, predict the distribution of $y[p]$. High anomaly score = the descriptor lands in a low-density region of the predicted distribution. The paper makes this explicit (§3.3):

"Anomaly scores $\{-\log q_{\theta^{\text{dens}}}(\mathbf{y}[p] \mid \mathbf{c}[p])\}_{p \in P}$ are position-wise prediction errors."

Design

Why does a diagonal Gaussian work for $q(y \mid c)$? Conditional densities should be simpler than marginals — once you've conditioned on enough context, the residual variation is small. Prior UVAS work used sin-cos positional encodings as $c$; that leaves a lot of variation un-explained at every voxel, so they needed a Glow flow to fit the residual distribution. Screener's masking-invariant conditions absorb so much "normal" variation (anatomy, patient age, contrast level — anything globally inferable) that the residual is approximately Gaussian. The ablation in §4 below shows this explicitly: with masking-invariant conditions, Gaussian and Glow are within 0.03–0.04 AUROC of each other across the four eval datasets.

The descriptor objective (App. B)

For completeness — though the density-model objective above is the "Screener loss" most readers want — here is DenseInfoNCE, the contrastive variant the paper uses for the descriptor:

$$\min_{\theta} \; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{k \in \{1,2\}} -\log \frac{\exp\!\bigl(\langle z_i^{(1)}, z_i^{(2)} \rangle / \tau\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(\langle z_i^{(1)}, z_i^{(2)} \rangle / \tau\bigr) + \sum_{j \ne i} \sum_{l \in \{1,2\}} \exp\!\bigl(\langle z_i^{(k)}, z_j^{(l)} \rangle / \tau\bigr)}$$

where $z_i^{(k)} = f(x^{(k)})[p_i^{(k)}]$ is the descriptor at the $i$-th sampled positive position $p_i$ in view $k$. The DenseVICReg variant swaps this for the VICReg objective (variance/invariance/covariance regularization); both reach comparable accuracy. The condition model uses the same objective, just with the masking augmentation added.

Distillation (§3.4)

A third loss — applied after the modular pipeline is trained — distills the entire stack into a single regression UNet $h_\phi$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x} \,\Bigl\|\, h_\phi(x) \;-\; \bigl(-\log q_{\theta^{\text{dens}}}\bigl(f_{\theta^{\text{desc}}}(x) \,\big|\, g_{\theta^{\text{cond}}}(x)\bigr)\bigr) \,\Bigr\|_2^2$$

Trained for one purpose: producing the score map in a single forward pass. The trained UNet weights are then used as self-supervised pretraining for downstream fine-tuning — replace the last conv with a per-task head, train on labeled data, and you have a model that beats Model Genesis, SwinUNETR, DAE, and ties VoCo at 25-case Dice (§4.2, Table 2).

Two things stand out. First, the gap between Screener's dense-SSL descriptor and ImageNet (0.96 vs 0.70) is 26 AUROC points on a 100-point scale — domain matters, even with an expressive density head. Second, STU-Net's 0.52 is the most damning result: supervised medical pretraining is worse than untargeted ImageNet pretraining. The supervised features have been pressured to predict organ boundaries; they've thrown away the noise that distinguishes "normal lung tissue with a slight density bump" from "lung cancer." The dense-SSL features keep that signal because they're optimized to discriminate voxels, not categories.

The pattern is clearest on LiTS but holds across all four datasets: Gaussian + masking-invariant condition is competitive with Glow + any condition. Glow gives a small additional bump (0.90 → 0.93 on LiTS) but at the cost of an invertible network with act-norm and affine couplings. The masking trick reduces the density model from a flow to two MLPs.

Note

Why "no condition + Glow" still gets 0.89 on LiTS. Glow can model multi-modal marginal distributions, so it doesn't strictly need a condition to perform well on tasks where the marginal is informative — and liver tumors happen to have an unusual descriptor distribution that even a marginal model can flag. The conditional advantage shows up on harder tasks (MIDRC, kidney) where the marginal distribution overlaps heavily between normal and pathological regions. This is also why the paper still publishes the full Screener with Glow + masking-invariance — the Gaussian variant is nearly matched, not fully matched.

§5 · The distillation move

Up to this point, Screener is an unsupervised UVAS method — useful for screening but not directly usable in a supervised pipeline. The modular three-model architecture isn't end-to-end differentiable, so you can't bolt a head on the end and fine-tune on labeled data. The distillation step (§3.4) addresses this.

Concretely: sample random image crops, pass them through the frozen modular Screener to obtain target score maps (negative log-density values), and train $h_\phi$ to predict those maps via MSE — last conv has one output channel, no activation. For downstream fine-tuning on binary pathology segmentation, replace the last conv with a single-channel head + sigmoid, then fine-tune on labeled cases with BCE + Dice loss. The fine-tuning results in Table 2 are the punchline: +49% Dice on LIDC over nnUNet trained from scratch (0.31 vs 0.21), competitive with VoCo (the strongest competing 3D medical SSL method), and beating Model Genesis / SwinUNETR / DAE comfortably.

The conceptual reframing is interesting: Screener's UVAS pipeline isn't just a screening tool, it's an SSL pretraining recipe. The score map is a rich, dense, anatomy-aware supervision signal; learning to reproduce it forces the UNet to encode everything Screener's modular stack encoded. And because that pretraining objective is unsupervised (you can compute the target score map on any unlabeled CT), it scales with unlabeled data the way modern SSL methods do.

Fine-tuning setting (Table 2): with 25 labeled cases per 3-fold and Wilcoxon signed-rank against random-init nnUNet as the baseline, Screener significantly improves on LIDC and LiTS (p < 0.01), matches on MIDRC, and ties VoCo at strongest performer across the board. Note the negative results too: SwinUNETR, Model Genesis, and DAE all degrade performance relative to random init on KiTS and LiTS — a useful reminder that SSL pretraining isn't a free lunch for downstream medical tasks, and that careful matching of pretraining objective to fine-tuning task matters.

Note

Why Dice scores under-estimate unsupervised Screener. Look at Table 1's Dice column: even Screener only hits 0.05 on LIDC and 0.10 on LiTS. That's not a bug — it's the evaluation protocol. The test masks only label the headline pathology (lung cancer for LIDC, pneumonia for MIDRC, kidney/liver tumors for KiTS/LiTS). Screener detects everything statistically rare, including unannotated pathologies in the same volumes. App. A is explicit about this: the pneumothorax in Fig. 1, correctly flagged by Screener, counts as a false positive against an incomplete mask. Voxel AUROC sidesteps the issue by sampling labeled-positive and out-of-mask-negative voxels separately. This is why the paper headlines AUROC, not Dice.

§7 · Limitations and what comes next

The honest limitations the paper acknowledges in §6:

  • The rarity assumption breaks for common pathologies. If a pathology is statistically common in the training distribution (say, COPD-related lung changes in an elderly cohort), Screener won't flag it. The implicit assumption is that "normal" patterns dominate; that's a clinical-practice assumption, not a guarantee.
  • Statistical anomalies aren't always clinical anomalies. Artifacts, scanner-specific noise, contrast injection patterns — all of these are "rare" relative to the typical training distribution and may show up as false positives. App. G's robustness study quantifies measurable but modest drops on low-dose vs high-dose and contrast vs non-contrast LIDC subsets.
  • CT-only validation. Transfer to MRI or X-ray is future work. The dense-SSL recipe should generalize, but the masking-invariance choice may not — MRI conditions could be more anatomically variable than CT in ways masking augmentation doesn't capture.
  • Scaling laws are open. The paper trains a fixed-size model on a fixed-size dataset. How AUROC scales with descriptor capacity, condition capacity, or unlabeled data size is explicitly listed as an open question.

What's not in the paper but follows from it:

The masking-invariance trick is general — any UVAS pipeline that conditions on positional context is a candidate. The natural-image UVAS literature (CFLow-AD, MSFlow) has spent years on better positional encodings; replacing those with masking-invariant SSL conditions and seeing what happens on MVTec-AD would be a one-paper experiment. The distillation move is also general — anywhere you have a complex modular unsupervised pipeline, distilling into a single regression network gives you an SSL pretraining recipe for free. The Screener authors land both of these as side observations; they're worth pulling out as standalone investigations.

Self-check

  1. Why does swapping ImageNet for a medically supervised encoder (STU-Net) worsen UVAS performance — AUROC 0.52 vs ImageNet's 0.70 on LIDC?
    Show answer

    Supervised pretraining objectives reward features that distinguish the supervised classes. STU-Net is trained on anatomical-structure segmentation, so its features become detectors for organ shapes. The textural "noise" that distinguishes normal lung tissue from a lung tumor doesn't help on the supervised task — it gets squeezed out. The density-based UVAS pipeline depends on exactly that texture-level discriminative signal: descriptors of normal vs pathological voxels need to lie in different regions of feature space. ImageNet pretraining, untargeted as it is, happens to preserve enough texture sensitivity to help. STU-Net throws it away. The lesson generalizes: any density-based UVAS method on top of a supervised feature extractor will inherit the extractor's blind spots.

  2. The condition model and descriptor model use the same UNet architecture and the same dense-SSL training procedure. What's the one engineering difference, and why is that single change so consequential?
    Show answer

    Random masking is added to the condition model's augmentation pipeline (along with crop + color jitter), and the binary mask is concatenated as a second input channel (App. F, code). The consequence: dense-SSL positive pairs for the condition model are voxel embeddings under different masking patterns of the same volume. The training pressure is "produce the same embedding at this anatomical position whether or not local content was masked out" — which is only achievable if the embedding is inferable from the unmasked context. Any local information (including pathologies) gets squeezed out. The condition $c[p]$ ends up encoding global anatomy, patient context, and tissue-type priors, but not local pathology — which is exactly what a conditional density model wants, because the conditioning variable shouldn't leak the variable it's predicting.

  3. With masking-invariant conditions, a diagonal Gaussian density model performs nearly as well as a Glow normalizing flow (Table 3). Why doesn't the flow's additional expressiveness translate into a larger gain here?
    Show answer

    Glow's value-add over a Gaussian is its ability to model multi-modal or heavy-tailed conditional distributions — situations where, given $c$, the descriptor $y$ has several distinct likely modes or a long tail of plausible values. Masking-invariant conditioning absorbs most of the things that would have caused multi-modality: anatomy, patient age, contrast level, scanner type. What's left after conditioning on $c$ is the residual variation in the descriptor at that exact anatomical position under fixed global context — and that residual is approximately Gaussian, because dense-SSL training pushes embeddings of similar normal voxels into tight clusters in feature space. Glow buys you 0.03–0.04 AUROC on average (Table 3), which is real but small. The trade-off the paper presents: pay a flow's complexity (act-norms, invertible 1×1 convs, affine couplings) for that 0.03, or accept a Gaussian and ship a simpler model.

  4. Why does the paper headline AUROC instead of Dice, even though segmentation papers conventionally report Dice?
    Show answer

    Dice requires a hard binary mask of "ground truth pathology vs not." The eval datasets only label one pathology each (lung cancer for LIDC, kidney tumor for KiTS, etc.). Other pathologies are present in the same volumes but unlabeled. Screener — by design — detects all rare patterns, not just the labeled ones. Every unannotated-but-correctly-flagged pathology counts as a false positive against the incomplete ground truth, depressing Dice systematically. App. A demonstrates this with a pneumothorax that Screener correctly localizes but that the ground-truth mask doesn't include. Voxel AUROC sidesteps the problem: sample 1000 labeled-pathology voxels (true positive rate) and 1000 out-of-mask "normal" voxels (false positive rate) per image; even if the out-of-mask "normal" pool contains some unannotated pathology, it's overwhelmingly dominated by truly normal tissue. The AUROC is therefore robust to mask incompleteness in a way Dice is not. The deeper point: when your method's outputs include "true positives not in the ground truth," your metric has to account for that or you'll undersell yourself.

  5. Screener makes a "rarity" assumption. Where does that break, concretely, and what would the failure look like in clinical practice?
    Show answer

    Two failure modes. (a) Common pathologies become false negatives. If the training distribution is enriched in a pathology — say, you train Screener on a cohort of COPD patients — then COPD-related lung changes are no longer statistically rare, so they map to high-density regions of $q(y \mid c)$ and get low anomaly scores. Clinically: Screener applied to a cohort with high pathology prevalence will miss the pathology it's most prevalent for. The paper doesn't quantify this directly but lists it as a limitation. (b) Non-clinical statistical anomalies become false positives. Anything rare-but-not-pathological — scanner artifacts, unusual contrast injection patterns, patient cohorts dramatically different from the training distribution — looks like an anomaly. App. G shows this happening on low-dose vs high-dose LIDC subsets: a measurable AUROC drop attributable to scanner-acquisition shifts, not to clinical content. In practice, you'd see Screener flagging "interesting" image regions that turn out to be artifacts on review. Both failure modes point to the same root cause: rarity is a statistical property of the training distribution, and clinical relevance is a property of the patient. They mostly align — but not always.
